Terra Bellum — four UI directions

Same scene in all four (France, 1804, Bas-Rhin selected), real Napoleonic map data from the game, 1920×1080, interactive: click departments of France, the End Turn control, and the map-lens switch.

What the research said

Charts have their own furniture. Graticule, graduated border outside the neatline (every nautical chart carries one), compass rose with rhumb lines, scale bar, title cartouche, heraldic devices. Bowen (1744): cartouche top-left, compass rose bottom-left. The furniture lives in the margin; the map stays clear.
A naval-chart game's lesson. Their first UI "felt like a schematic displayed onto a screen"; the fix was to push clutter (clock etc.) to the chart's edges and treat the interface as analog instruments on a physical table.
Pentiment. Its UI feels historical because it is built from print culture (typefaces carry meaning, paper shows through colour) rather than by skinning a modern layout.
Humankind critique. Players said translucent, un-bordered widgets and non-physical animation read as sci-fi, not historical.
Selective diegesis. Not every control needs to be an object; plain, quiet overlays are fine for minor controls.
This project's own brief. "The calm, slightly dangerous focus of a staff officer at a map table at night." Materials: laid paper, ink, brass, umber, wax. The player loves the title screen: turning globe in a graduated bezel ring. Decoration has a budget; the map is the hero; orders happen on the map.

Diagnosis of the earlier attempts: they borrowed the layout of other strategy games and the look of a web dashboard (rounded rectangles, flat chips) and then tinted it. Shapes and colour were never derived from one object world. Each direction below picks one object world and builds every control from it.

Sources consulted: IHO chart specification S-4 / INT2 (graduation of borders); Royal Museums Greenwich chart records (graduated borders on 17th–18th c. charts); Toups Dugas et al., "Paper to pixels: a chronicle of map interfaces in games" (DIS 2020); Pentiment art-direction interviews (Inverse, Game Developer); the Atlantic 41 tactical-chart devlog (as summarised by search); this repo's design/game-brief.md and art-bible.md.

1 · Neatline

The screen is a chart sheet.

The graduated degree/minute border of an Admiralty chart frames the game and is a real scale (it lines up with the map). Resources sit in a "scale bar" strip, the nation is a cartouche, the lens switch is a legend of references, End Turn is a compass rose whose needle swings one point each turn, and the province inspector is a paper slip tied to the province by a leader line.

  • Strongest "this is a map" identity, cheapest to build in Godot (lines, rectangles, text).
  • Square corners, engraved rules, dash-dot national boundaries and coast water-lining come straight from real charts.
  • Risk: light paper slips on a dark map are high contrast, so the inspector competes with the map.

2 · Bezel

The title screen's instrument language, carried into the game.

Everything is a brass-bezelled dial: the nation medallion, four arc gauges for resources, a month-ring date dial, the minimap as a mini-globe in a graduated ring, a radio-tuner strip for map lenses, and a chronometer for End Turn. The six menu entries sit on an arc. Selecting a province opens a radial command ring ON the map (verbs at the compass points, army number in the hub) with a small info tag - orders happen on the map.

  • Closest to the title screen the player loves; most "game-like".
  • Radial ring puts verbs where the player is looking and shrinks the side panel to a tag.
  • Risk: more custom drawing (arcs, ticks) and the ring must avoid map labels/tokens; denser at 390 px.

3 · Almanac

A printed book beside a night map.

No boxes. The right 590 px is a cream almanac page: running head, rubricated drop-cap title, ledger rows with dotted leaders, hatched scale bars, orders as an italic contents list with a pointing-hand marker, costs as footnotes, dispatches as § notes, and End Turn as a red rubber stamp. The map is an engraving: hatch per nation, ruled sea, masthead with a dateline of figures.

  • The most distinctive and the most legible (typography does the hierarchy, not frames).
  • Cheap: Labels, lines and one paper panel; old-style figures from Alegreya.
  • Risk: a large light page beside a dark map; on phones the "page" becomes a bottom sheet.

4 · Staff Table

A night desk: brass, wood, wax, paper.

The map lies on a leather desk under a lamp. Nameplate, odometer-drum counters, a wooden tool rack with pressable brass keys, a manila dossier with typed figures and a paperclip, pinned notes for alerts, a wax seal for End Turn that cracks when pressed.

  • Most tactile and charming; matches "staff officer at a map table at night" literally.
  • Risk: this is the "too decorative" end of the range - the project brief says decoration has a budget. Needs textures or many drawn gradients in Godot.
  • Good source of single ideas: odometer drums, wax seal, pinned notes.

My recommendation

Build 2 · Bezel as the skeleton (it is the title screen's language, so menu and game finally feel like one game, and the radial ring is the most game-like interaction). Borrow from 3 · Almanac for the detail views (dossier / nation card / Annals as typeset pages with leader dots and footnoted costs), from 1 · Neatline for the real graduated frame on the map edge and the dash-dot national borders, and one prop from 4 (wax seal or odometer drums) at most. Everything else stays flat.

Questions that would change the plan: (1) light page beside a dark map (Almanac) vs. all-dark (Bezel) for text-heavy screens; (2) is a radial ring acceptable on touch (thumb reach, 44 px targets), or should verbs stay in a list; (3) how much custom drawing are you willing to maintain in Godot (arcs and ticks are cheap; textured wood/leather are not).
